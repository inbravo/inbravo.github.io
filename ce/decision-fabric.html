Emerging Category · October 2026

The Meta-Decision Layer: Decision Models

A new class of small, specialized models has emerged in 2026 — not to answer questions, but to decide how questions should be answered. These decision models sit above all six Decision Fabric planes, making typed per-turn judgments about context visibility, model routing, tool selection, and permission before the frontier model is ever called.

TypeSafe
Jev 1.13
typesafe/jev-1.13

The original decision model. Makes typed visibility decisions (hidden / short / long / full) per context chunk per query, routes to cheap or frontier models, selects tool schemas in tiers. State is explicit, addressable chunks — not an append-only transcript.

Jared Palmer
Kev 4B
jaredpalmer/kev-4b

Open-weights 4B decision model. Implements the Jev pattern as an independently deployable model artifact — runs on-premise or in any cloud. No TypeSafe dependency.

Respan

respan/span-01

Decision and routing model with a Lite variant for cost-sensitive deployments. Free tier available. Targets latency-sensitive agentic pipelines where per-turn decision overhead must stay under 50ms.

Upstage
Solar Decide
upstage/solar-decide

Built on Upstage's Solar model family. Specialises in classification, scoring, and routing decisions — positioning it closer to Databricks ai_decide() and OpenAI Decisions API than to pure context-visibility use cases.

Inception

inception/mercury-decide

Free tier available. Mercury Decide is Inception's entry into the decision model space, designed to pair with their Mercury diffusion-based generation models for ultra-low-latency agent pipelines.

togethercomputer/tev1-4b-experimental

Experimental release from Together. 4B parameter class. Available via Together's inference API alongside open-weights model hosting — targets teams that want to fine-tune the decision model on their own routing patterns.

Where decision models sit relative to the six planes

Decision models are not infrastructure for any single plane — they are the orchestration layer above all six. Per turn, a decision model answers: which context chunks does the frontier model need to see (Fact Plane visibility)? Which policies apply to this request (Policy Plane routing)? Does the calling agent have the right to invoke this tool (Authority Plane gate)? Which precedents are relevant (Experience Plane retrieval)? What is the declared objective (Intent Plane activation)? These are meta-decisions over the planes, not decisions within them. The frontier model receives only what the decision model permits — context assembled on purpose, not accumulated by accident.

The market signal: commodity layer, institutional moat

Six competing decision models shipped within 90 days of Jev's release. When a pattern commoditises that fast, the model itself is not the moat. Differentiation moves up — to the institutional knowledge each plane encodes: the policies, authority schema, precedent store, and intent library the decision model reasons over. And it moves down — to the Decision Contract, the machine-readable evidence package that proves the judgment was made correctly under the right authority against the right policy. None of the five platforms assessed below, and none of the six decision models above, address either layer. That is the build opportunity.

Platform integration note — all five platforms assessed below can host a decision model via their agent frameworks (AgentCore Gateway, Databricks Agent Bricks, Azure AI Foundry, Snowflake Cortex AI Gateway, open-source LangGraph). The model tier is not where the platforms differentiate; the institutional knowledge planes are.

The combination of Keycloak token exchange + LangGraph interrupt + Temporal approval signals provides a fully composable authority model. Temporal's durable execution guarantees that approval workflows survive failures, network partitions, and long waits — no other platform offers this durability for approval gates. The LangGraph + Temporal integration pattern is documented and production-tested. Verdict. The open source ecosystem offers the most flexibility and the deepest individual components — OPA for policy-as-code, Temporal for durable approval workflows, Langfuse for trace capture, spLink for entity resolution — but no platform integration means every enterprise must assemble and operate these separately, incurring significant engineering overhead. The Authority Plane combination of Keycloak + Temporal is the most durable approval-workflow architecture available. Critically, the open data standards layer (Bitol/ODCS, ODPS, dbt Contracts, Soda Core, W3C PROV, OpenLineage) operates at the data trust layer, not the judgment trust layer — they are the right building blocks for a Decision Contract's Fact evidence, but they stop short of the judgment plane. This is precisely the whitespace the Decision Fabric addresses: assembling ODCS-compliant data facts, OPA-evaluated policies, Keycloak-issued authority, Langfuse-captured experience, and declared intent into a single machine-readable evidence package at judgment time. Two planes (Intent, packaged Decision Contract) remain absent even combining every available tool.

Cross-Platform Gap Summary

Capabilities absent on ALL five platforms — genuine build opportunities as of October 2026

Intent Plane

Structured Decision Objective Schema

No platform offers a machine-readable, versioned schema for declaring agent objectives, priorities, and trade-off surfaces. Intent lives in free-text system prompts on every platform. MIGT taxonomy and KYA framework (arXiv 2026) describe the requirement but no vendor or OSS project has shipped a production implementation. This is the largest gap in enterprise AI decision governance.

Intent Plane

Reusable Intent Library

No platform offers reusable, versioned intent templates that can be applied across agents and decisions. Every agent team hand-writes objectives from scratch. No shared primitives for common decision patterns (maximize return subject to risk constraint, minimize cost subject to service-level constraint) exist in any vendor catalog or open standard.

Experience Plane

Decision Record with Outcome Linkage

All five platforms store conversational context or raw traces, but none link a specific decision record to its downstream business outcome (e.g., "this credit approval → loan performance 180 days later"). Without outcome linkage, there is no feedback loop for improving agent decision quality or auditing systematic bias over time. TrajectoryDB (arXiv Sep 2026) proposes the data structure but is not yet production software.

Experience Plane

Precedent Retrieval with Bias Filtering

No platform offers comparable-case retrieval optimized for governance decisions with quality and bias filtering. Vector stores (Weaviate, pgvector, Cortex Search) can retrieve similar records, but no platform provides the scoring layer that filters out low-quality, stale, or demographically biased precedents before they are used to ground a new decision.

Policy Plane

Regulatory Rule Ingestion

No platform can ingest regulatory text (Basel III, GDPR, EU AI Act articles) and produce versioned, executable policy rules. Every platform requires manual translation from legal/regulatory text into Cedar, Rego, or service policy functions. This is a significant compliance engineering cost in financial services and healthcare, and a source of translation errors with regulatory risk.

Authority Plane

Delegated Decision-Rights Schema

No platform offers a native construct for declaring "agent X may approve decisions of type Y up to threshold Z, subject to conditions W." AWS Cedar + Step Functions comes closest but requires hand-coding. The absence of this primitive means approval threshold enforcement is always custom-built, untested as a governance primitive, and invisible to audit tooling.

Decision Contract

Packaged Machine-Readable Evidence Package

No platform produces a single machine-readable evidence package for a specific judgment event that assembles the grounding facts used, the policy version evaluated against, the agent authority and delegation chain, the comparable precedents consulted, and the declared objectives. The W3C CG AI Agent Protocol issue #34 (2026) names this the "auditability gap." NovaFabric (arXiv) describes the cryptographic architecture but has not shipped as a product on any platform.

Decision Contract

Inference-Time Cryptographic Signing

No production platform cryptographically signs the inference event at the moment of judgment — capturing what the agent was given, what authority it operated under, and what it concluded, in a tamper-evident record. CloudTrail, Purview, and MLflow all create post-hoc records that could theoretically be reconstructed, but none provide a forward-signed cryptographic proof of the inference-time state. This is the evidentiary standard required for high-stakes regulated decisions under full EU AI Act enforcement (Aug 2026).
